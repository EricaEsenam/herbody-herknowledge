What Is Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)?

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a common long-term hormonal condition that affects the reproductive system as well as other aspects of health. It affects approximately 10–13% of women of reproductive age worldwide, with up to 70% of affected women remaining undiagnosed.

PCOS could affect hormone levels, menstrual cycles, ovulation, fertility, metabolism, skin, and mental health. Its symptoms and effects vary from person to person.

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a common yet under-diagnosed hormonal condition that affects approximately 1 in 15 women worldwide, leading to a wide range of physical and emotional challenges. Despite its prevalence, many women with PCOS face difficulties in diagnosis and management due to a lack of awareness among healthcare providers and the general public.

Common Symptoms

Common features associated with PCOS include:

  • Irregular or absent menstrual periods
  • Irregular or absent ovulation
  • Difficulty becoming pregnant
  • Increased facial or body hair
  • Acne or oily skin
  • Hair thinning or hair loss
  • Weight-related and metabolic changes

Not everyone experiences the same symptoms, and their severity can vary.

Other Health Risks

PCOS is also associated with an increased risk of several longer-term health problems, including:

  • Insulin resistance
  • Type 2 diabetes
  • High blood pressure
  • Abnormal cholesterol or other blood-fat levels
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Mental-health concerns

Because of these effects, PCOS is understood as more than a reproductive condition. It can affect several areas of a person's health throughout life.

How Is PCOS Diagnosed?

Diagnosis involves assessing a person's symptoms, medical history, and appropriate clinical investigations.

In adults, PCOS is generally identified when at least two of three features are present after other possible causes have been excluded:

  1. Irregular or absent ovulation, often reflected by irregular menstrual cycles.
  2. Evidence of increased androgen activity, either through symptoms or laboratory testing.
  3. Polycystic ovarian morphology identified through appropriate imaging.

Having an ovarian appearance associated with PCOS alone does not necessarily mean that a person has the condition.

Treatment and Management

There is no single treatment that applies to everyone with PCOS. Management depends on the person's symptoms, health needs, and reproductive goals.

Management can include approaches to:

  • Regulate menstrual cycles
  • Manage androgen-related symptoms
  • Improve metabolic health
  • Support fertility when pregnancy is desired
  • Address mental-health concerns
  • Reduce longer-term health risks

Professional medical care is important because PCOS can affect several aspects of health.

Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS)

Although PCOS became the widely recognized name for the condition, scientific and clinical understanding of the condition continued to develop. It became increasingly clear that the condition was much more complex than its name suggested. The term “polycystic” could lead people to believe that the condition was mainly about ovarian cysts, while the condition involved hormonal, metabolic, reproductive, skin, and mental-health aspects.

As a result, an international process was undertaken to reconsider the name.

The condition is now known as: Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS)

PMOS is the new name for the condition previously known as PCOS. The name change followed a 14-year international collaboration involving researchers, healthcare professionals, patient representatives, and more than 50 patient and professional organizations, including the Endocrine Society. The process included more than 22,000 survey responses and international workshops involving people with lived experience and multidisciplinary health professionals.

Why Was the Name Changed?

1. The Condition Is Not Simply About "Cysts"

The term "polycystic" could create the impression that people with the condition have abnormal cysts on their ovaries. However, the condition is not simply characterized by ovarian cysts. The ovarian features are only one part of a much broader condition.

2. The Condition Affects More Than the Ovaries

PCOS can affect several areas of health, including:

  • Hormonal and endocrine function
  • Metabolic health
  • Reproductive health
  • Skin and hair
  • Mental health
  • Long-term health

The proposed new name therefore places greater emphasis on the hormonal and metabolic dimensions of the condition.

3. The Old Name Could Contribute to Misunderstanding

The previous name could contribute to misunderstanding among patients and the wider public about what the condition involves. This could contribute to delayed recognition, missed diagnoses, and inadequate understanding of the condition.

4. PMOS Better Reflects the Complexity of the Condition

The name Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome is intended to communicate that the condition involves multiple interconnected aspects of health rather than being solely an ovarian disorder.

  • Polyendocrine: Highlights the hormonal and endocrine aspects.
  • Metabolic: Highlights metabolic effects and associated health risks.
  • Ovarian: Retains the reproductive and ovarian aspects of the condition.

What Does the Name Change Mean?

The change from PCOS to PMOS does not mean that the underlying condition has suddenly become a different disease. Rather, the new terminology is intended to provide a more accurate description of the condition that has previously been called PCOS. Existing knowledge about its symptoms, diagnosis, management, and associated health risks therefore remains important during the transition to the new terminology. The international transition to PMOS is being supported through education and awareness initiatives, with the new terminology expected to be fully incorporated into the 2028 International Guideline update.

PMOS Today

PMOS is recognized as a complex, long-term condition involving hormonal, metabolic, reproductive, and other aspects of health. Understanding the transition from PCOS to PMOS helps patients, families, healthcare professionals, researchers, and the wider public better understand what the condition involves and why its name has changed.

Important Notice

Anyone experiencing persistent menstrual irregularities, fertility difficulties, symptoms of increased androgen activity, or other concerns should seek advice from a qualified healthcare professional.
