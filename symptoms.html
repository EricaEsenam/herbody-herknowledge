Understanding the Symptoms

Learn about the symptoms associated with Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) and Endometriosis. Understanding these symptoms can help people recognize when they need to seek professional healthcare.

Understanding the Signs

Symptoms associated with PCOS and Endometriosis can vary in type and severity. Some individuals may experience several symptoms, while others may experience only a few or no noticeable symptoms.

The presence of one or more symptoms does not by itself confirm a diagnosis. Proper assessment by a qualified healthcare professional is required.

PCOS Symptoms

The symptoms of Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) vary among individuals depending on their hormonal profile, ovulatory function, and other associated health factors. Some individuals experience several symptoms, while others experience only a few noticeable signs. Symptoms can develop gradually and change over time.

Irregular Menstrual Periods

PCOS can disrupt the normal process of ovulation, resulting in irregular menstrual cycles. Individuals can experience infrequent periods, unpredictable menstrual intervals, or the absence of menstruation for several months. These changes are often associated with irregular or absent ovulation. When ovulation does not occur regularly, menstrual cycles become unpredictable, and the uterine lining may not shed at regular intervals.

Changes in Menstrual Flow

PCOS can be associated with changes in the amount, duration, or frequency of menstrual bleeding. When ovulation occurs infrequently, the uterine lining can remain under hormonal stimulation for an extended period. This sometimes results in irregular, prolonged, or heavier bleeding. However, menstrual bleeding patterns differ among individuals, and these changes are not specific to PCOS alone.

Hormonal Changes

PCOS is commonly associated with increased androgen levels or signs of increased androgen activity. Androgens are hormones present in all individuals, although their levels and effects differ. Increased androgen activity in PCOS can contribute to acne, excess facial or body hair, and thinning hair on the scalp. Many individuals with PCOS also experience irregular ovulation. Insulin resistance is common in PCOS and can contribute to increased androgen production in some individuals.

Skin Changes

Hormonal changes associated with PCOS can contribute to acne and increased oil production in the skin. Higher androgen activity can stimulate the sebaceous glands, which produce sebum, an oily substance that helps protect the skin. Excess sebum can contribute to blocked pores and acne. Some individuals also develop darkened, thickened areas of skin, particularly around the neck, armpits, or groin. This change, known as acanthosis nigricans, is associated with insulin resistance.

Changes in Hair Growth

Changes in androgen activity can affect hair growth in different ways. Some individuals with PCOS experience hirsutism, which refers to excessive coarse hair growth in areas such as the chin, upper lip, chest, abdomen, or back. Others experience androgen-related thinning of scalp hair, particularly around the crown or central parting. The presence and severity of these changes differ among individuals.

Other Symptoms

PCOS is also associated with metabolic changes, including insulin resistance and an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes. Some individuals experience weight gain or changes in weight over time, although PCOS affects individuals of different body sizes. Sleep disturbances and psychological concerns, including anxiety and depression, also occur more frequently among people with PCOS. These associated concerns differ among individuals and do not occur in everyone with the condition.

The presence, combination, and severity of symptoms differ from one individual to another. Not everyone with PCOS experiences all these symptoms, and some individuals have few obvious external signs. Symptoms alone cannot establish a diagnosis; assessment by a qualified healthcare professional is necessary.

Symptoms of Endometriosis

The symptoms of endometriosis vary depending on the location of the affected tissue, the extent of the condition, and individual experiences of pain. Some individuals experience severe symptoms, while others experience mild symptoms or none at all. The severity of symptoms does not always correspond to the extent of the condition.

Painful Menstrual Periods

Endometriosis can cause significant menstrual pain, also known as dysmenorrhoea. The pain may begin before menstruation, continue during the period, or persist afterwards. In some cases, it can interfere with daily activities, school, work, and sleep.

Pelvic Pain Outside Menstruation

Some individuals experience pelvic pain outside their menstrual periods. This pain may be persistent or intermittent and can be associated with inflammation, irritation, or scar tissue within the pelvis. However, pelvic pain can have other causes and requires appropriate assessment.

Pain During or After Sexual Intercourse

Pain during or after sexual intercourse, particularly deep pain, may occur in individuals with endometriosis. It can be associated with lesions or inflammation affecting tissues and structures within the pelvis.

Painful Urination

Pain or discomfort during urination may occur when endometriosis affects areas near the bladder or urinary tract. The discomfort may become more noticeable during menstruation. Urinary infections and other conditions can also cause painful urination, so professional assessment is important.

Pain When Passing Stool

Pain during bowel movements may occur when endometriosis affects tissues near the bowel or rectum. The pain may become worse during menstruation. Some individuals may also experience bloating, constipation, or diarrhoea, although these symptoms can have other causes.

Blood in the Urine

Blood in the urine, known as haematuria, is an uncommon symptom that may occur when endometriosis affects the bladder or urinary tract. Blood in the urine should not automatically be attributed to endometriosis and requires medical assessment.

Rectal Bleeding

Rectal bleeding is uncommon but may occur in some cases where endometriosis affects the bowel or rectum. Because rectal bleeding can have several possible causes, it should be assessed by a healthcare professional.

Fatigue

Fatigue is commonly reported by individuals with endometriosis. Ongoing pain, disrupted sleep, and the effects of living with a chronic condition may contribute to tiredness. Heavy menstrual bleeding and iron deficiency may also contribute in some cases.

Difficulty Becoming Pregnant

Endometriosis is associated with fertility difficulties in some individuals. Inflammation, scar tissue, and changes affecting the ovaries or fallopian tubes may contribute. However, having endometriosis does not automatically mean a person will experience infertility; many individuals with the condition become pregnant.

Other Cyclical Symptoms

Some symptoms may repeatedly appear or become worse at particular stages of the menstrual cycle. These may include pelvic discomfort, bowel or urinary symptoms, and fatigue. Recording when symptoms occur can help a healthcare professional assess the pattern, although cyclical symptoms alone do not confirm endometriosis.

Symptoms Outside the Pelvis

In rare cases, endometriosis can affect areas outside the pelvis, including the diaphragm, chest, abdominal wall, or surgical scars. Symptoms depend on the affected location. Chest pain or breathing difficulties that occur around menstruation require medical attention, and severe chest pain or difficulty breathing requires urgent medical care.

When Should Professional Care Be Sought?

Professional healthcare should be sought when pain is severe, persistent, recurrent, or interferes with daily activities. Assessment is also important for pain during urination or bowel movements, unexplained blood in the urine or rectal bleeding, persistent fatigue, or difficulty becoming pregnant.

Keeping a record of symptoms, their severity, and when they occur during the menstrual cycle may help during a healthcare consultation.

Important Reminder

Symptoms alone cannot confirm Endometriosis or Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS). Although some symptoms may overlap, these are different conditions that require appropriate assessment.
