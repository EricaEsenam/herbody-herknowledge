Understanding Endometriosis

Learn about endometriosis, its symptoms, diagnosis, and management.

What Is Endometriosis?

Endometriosis is a chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus, known as endometrial-like tissue, is found outside the uterus.

This tissue can trigger a chronic inflammatory reaction, which may result in pain and adhesions. Adhesions are bands of scar tissue that can cause organs or other structures to attach to one another.

The symptoms of endometriosis can change throughout the menstrual cycle because hormonal levels fluctuate. For many people, symptoms may become worse shortly before or during menstruation.

While some people experience severe pelvic pain, others may have few or no symptoms and may mistake their symptoms for ordinary menstrual pain.

How Common Is Endometriosis?

Endometriosis is estimated to affect approximately 2–10% of women in the general population. Among women experiencing infertility, an estimated 30–50% may have endometriosis.

The condition can have a significant effect on quality of life. Pain and discomfort may interfere with normal daily activities and may also be associated with anxiety and depression.

What Causes Endometriosis?

The exact cause of endometriosis remains unknown. Several theories have been proposed, but none has been completely proven.

One commonly discussed theory is retrograde menstruation, where tissue from the uterus moves through the fallopian tubes into the abdominal cavity during menstruation and may develop into endometriotic lesions.

Estrogen is also considered important in the development and activity of endometriosis. Other possible explanations include genetic and immune-system factors.

Currently, there are no known methods that can reliably prevent endometriosis, and there are no genetic tests that can predict whether someone will develop the condition in the future.

Common Symptoms

Endometriosis can cause different symptoms from person to person. Common symptoms include:

  • Painful menstrual periods
  • Pelvic pain that occurs outside menstruation
  • Pain during or after sexual intercourse
  • Painful urination
  • Pain when passing stool
  • Blood in the urine or rectal bleeding
  • Fatigue
  • Infertility
  • Other symptoms that repeatedly occur in connection with the menstrual cycle

Symptoms that occur in a pattern related to menstruation are described as cyclical symptoms.

Severe pain can be particularly important to recognize when it prevents someone from carrying out normal daily activities.

How Is Endometriosis Diagnosed?

Symptoms can provide an important clue, but symptoms alone cannot confirm endometriosis because many of them can occur with other conditions.

A healthcare professional may use:

  • Medical history and assessment of symptoms
  • Clinical examination
  • Ultrasound
  • MRI(Magnetic Resonance Imaging)
  • Laparoscopy in selected circumstances

What Is Laparoscopy?

Laparoscopy is a type of minimally invasive surgery, sometimes called keyhole surgery. During the procedure, a surgeon makes small incisions in the abdomen and inserts a thin instrument with a camera, called a laparoscope. The camera allows the surgeon to examine organs and tissues inside the abdomen and pelvis.

Laparoscopy can help identify endometriosis. When appropriate, the surgeon may also remove or treat endometriosis-related lesions during the same procedure. Whether surgery is suitable depends on the person's symptoms, clinical findings, preferences, and treatment needs.

Although laparoscopy has traditionally been an important method for diagnosing endometriosis, surgery is not necessary in every case. A healthcare professional may use a person's symptoms, clinical assessment, and imaging tests, such as ultrasound or MRI, to guide diagnosis and treatment.

WHO Guidance: Treatment Does Not Always Require Surgery(Laparoscopy)

The World Health Organization (WHO) explains that endometriosis can be diagnosed clinically using a person's symptoms and appropriate non-invasive imaging, such as ultrasound or MRI. Surgery is not necessarily required before treatment begins.

This approach can allow people to access appropriate pain management and hormonal treatments without requiring surgery solely to establish a diagnosis. Earlier clinical recognition may also help reduce delays in receiving appropriate care.

Types of Endometriosis

Endometriosis can occur in different forms depending on where the lesions are located. The three main types described in the ESHRE guideline are:

1. Superficial Peritoneal Endometriosis

This is the most common type. The lesions occur on the peritoneum, the thin layer covering the inner surfaces of the pelvic cavity.

2. Ovarian Endometriosis

This occurs when endometriosis affects the ovaries and forms an endometrioma, sometimes called a "chocolate cyst."

3. Deep Endometriosis

This is a less common form in which endometriosis extends deeply into tissues beneath the surface of the peritoneum or involves pelvic organs.

More than one type can occur in the same person.

Endometriosis and Pain

Pain is one of the major concerns associated with endometriosis. Because endometriosis is a chronic condition, treatment focuses on reducing symptoms and improving quality of life.

The appropriate treatment can differ from one person to another. Factors considered include the severity and type of pain, fertility concerns, whether pregnancy is desired, age, treatment effectiveness, possible side effects, and the type of endometriosis.

Treatment and Management

There is currently no cure that completely eliminates endometriosis, but treatment can help reduce symptoms.

Treatment options may include:

  • Pain-relieving medication
  • Hormonal treatments
  • Surgical treatment

Hormonal treatments can be considered for managing endometriosis-associated pain. Surgery may also be considered in appropriate cases, particularly when endometriotic lesions are identified or when symptoms do not improve with other treatments.

Treatment decisions should be made together with a qualified healthcare professional because the most appropriate approach depends on the individual's symptoms, fertility plans, type of disease, and treatment preferences.

Endometriosis and Fertility

Having endometriosis does not automatically mean that a person is infertile. The ESHRE guideline estimates that approximately 60–70% of women with endometriosis are fertile and can become pregnant spontaneously.

However, some people with endometriosis experience difficulties becoming pregnant and may require fertility support.

Pregnancy should not be considered a treatment or cure for endometriosis. People who want to become pregnant should discuss their options with a healthcare professional.

For people experiencing fertility difficulties, options may include surgical treatment in selected cases or medically assisted reproduction such as Intrauterine Insemination (IUI) or In vitro fertilization(IVF) .

Endometriosis in Adolescents

Endometriosis can also occur in adolescents. Possible signs include:

  • Chronic or recurring pelvic pain
  • Particularly painful menstrual periods
  • Pain when passing stool or urinating
  • Cyclical pelvic pain
  • Missing school because of symptoms

When pain is severe enough to interfere with normal activities, it is important to discuss the symptoms with a healthcare professional.

When to Seek Professional Care

Persistent or severe menstrual or pelvic pain should not simply be dismissed as normal menstrual discomfort.

A healthcare professional should be consulted when symptoms such as severe menstrual pain, recurring pelvic pain, painful bowel movements or urination, cyclical symptoms, fatigue, or fertility difficulties are affecting daily life.

Keeping a symptom diary can also help a person record when symptoms occur and discuss them more clearly with a healthcare professional.

Important Notice

This information is provided for health education and awareness purposes only. It is not intended to diagnose endometriosis or replace professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment.

If you experience persistent or severe symptoms, speak with a qualified healthcare professional for appropriate assessment and care.
